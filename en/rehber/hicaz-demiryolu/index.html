Hejaz Railway Station and Museum, Madinah

محطة سكة حديد الحجاز

The last stop of Sultan Abdülhamid II's Damascus-Madinah line, and the Anbariyah Mosque

Overview

The Hejaz Railway Station in Madinah is in the al-Anbariyah district, about 1 km south-west of the Prophet's Mosque. It is the last stop of the Hejaz Railway, some 1,300 km long, laid from Damascus to Madinah by order of the Ottoman Sultan Abdülhamid II. Opened in 1908, the line cut the journey from Damascus to Madinah, which pilgrims had made in about forty days, to five.

The station complex included a passenger building, warehouses and workshops. The Anbariyah Mosque right next to it was built by Abdülhamid II together with the station in the same year. Built of black basalt, with two slender Ottoman minarets and one large dome, it is today one of the finest examples of Ottoman architecture in Madinah. The restored station building opened in 2006 as the Hejaz Railway Museum, displaying old rails, locomotives, carriages and station equipment.

History

For centuries the long, exhausting and dangerous caravan journeys of pilgrims from Damascus to Madinah and Makkah had been one of the main concerns of the Ottoman state. By a decree of 2 May 1900, Abdülhamid II ordered a railway to be built from Damascus to the Hejaz. Construction began with a ceremony on 1 September 1900, the anniversary of the Sultan's accession. There were also plans to extend the line from Madinah to Makkah and on to Jeddah and Yemen.

The estimated cost was about 4 million lira, more than eighteen per cent of the state budget of 1901. Abdülhamid II opened the donation campaign with a personal gift of 50,000 lira. Donations came from every corner of the Ottoman lands and from across the Islamic world, from India to Egypt; donors were given nickel, silver and gold Hejaz Railway medals. Supported also by deductions from officials' salaries, stamps and special revenues, the project was seen as a common work of the Ummah.

Technical management was given in 1901 to the German engineer Meissner; forty-three engineers took part, most of them Ottoman, German and Italian. Most of the labour was provided by Ottoman soldiers, up to 7,500 of them. Over time the engineering work passed increasingly to Muslim Ottoman engineers. The line reached Amman in 1903, Ma'an in 1904 and al-Ula in 1907, and was opened at a ceremony in Madinah on 1 September 1908. A narrow gauge of 1.05 m was used to reduce costs.

The plan to extend it to Makkah, however, was never realised because of the opposition of Sharif Husayn, the Emir of Makkah, and the tribes of the region. In the First World War, after Sharif Husayn's revolt in June 1916, raids guided by British officers repeatedly destroyed the line. The railway was Madinah's only supply route. Fahreddin Pasha defended the besieged city for two years and seven months; he sent the sacred relics kept at the Prophet's Chamber to Istanbul, and was forced to surrender the city on 10 January 1919, months after the Armistice of Mudros. After the war the Saudi section of the line never operated again.

Key Events

  1. 2 May 1900

    Abdülhamid II ordered the construction of the Hejaz Railway.

  2. 1 September 1900

    Construction began with a ceremony on the anniversary of the Sultan's accession.

  3. 1903-1907

    The line reached Amman (1903), Ma'an (1904) and al-Ula (1907).

  4. 1 September 1908

    The Madinah station was opened; the Anbariyah Mosque beside it was built the same year.

  5. 1916-1919

    The line was destroyed by raids; Fahreddin Pasha defended Madinah, and the city was surrendered on 10 January 1919.

  6. 2006

    The restored station opened as the Hejaz Railway Museum.

Religious Significance

This is a historic site rather than a place of religious visitation. Yet the Hejaz Railway was built with the intention of serving the pilgrims, the guests of Allah, and making it easier to reach the Two Holy Sanctuaries, and it rose thanks to donations from every corner of the Islamic world. In this sense it is a symbol of Muslims' love for the Haramayn and of their striving together for good.

For visitors from Turkey the station also recalls Fahreddin Pasha, who defended Madinah to the very end, and the sacrifice of the Ottoman soldiers. A visit is an occasion to draw lessons from history and to remember the effort made for a good cause.

Etiquette and Tips

  • The station is about a 15-20 minute walk from the Prophet's Mosque; in the hot hours prefer a taxi or the city sightseeing bus.
  • The Anbariyah Mosque is open for the daily prayers; you can time your visit for a prayer to see its Ottoman architecture. Friday prayer is not held there.
  • Even if the museum is closed, the station buildings and the old locomotives displayed in the park around it can be seen from outside.
  • When taking photos, do not disturb the congregation or people praying.
