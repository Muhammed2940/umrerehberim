Historic Jeddah (Al-Balad)

جدة التاريخية

“The Gate to Makkah”: UNESCO World Heritage coral-stone houses

Overview

Jeddah, on the Red Sea coast about 70 km from Makkah, is one of the largest cities in Saudi Arabia. The city's historic core, popularly called “Al-Balad” (the town), was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2014 as “Historic Jeddah, the Gate to Makkah”.

Al-Balad is known for its multi-storey houses, some over 30 metres high, built of coral-based stone (manqabi) quarried from the coastal lagoons and reinforced with timber beams, and for the carved wooden bay windows called “rawasheen”. The area includes the al-Mazloum, al-Sham, al-Yemen and al-Bahr quarters; historic mosques such as the al-Shafi'i Mosque and the Uthman ibn Affan (Ebony) Mosque; old souqs; and Naseef House, now used as a museum.

History

Very little is known about Jeddah before Islam. The city's importance grew when, during the caliphate of Uthman (26 AH / 647), Jeddah was chosen as the port of Makkah in place of the harbour of Shu'aybah to the south. From then on, pilgrims arriving by sea from North Africa, Egypt and India landed at Jeddah on their way to Makkah.

Medieval travellers mention the city's walls, stone inns and mosques. In the Mamluk period a fortress was built in 1511 by Husayn al-Kurdi against Portuguese attacks, and the city was enclosed by a wall. Jeddah came under Ottoman rule in 1517, and a Portuguese attack in 1541 was repelled. In the Ottoman period the city became the seat of a sanjak, and Kara Mustafa Pasha brought water from afar and built an inn, a bathhouse and a mosque.

According to some narrations, a grave said to belong to Eve (Hawwa) used to be visited in Jeddah; however, this is not established by any authentic source, and the grave was demolished in 1928.

After the siege of 1925 Jeddah passed under Saudi rule. The walls surrounding the city were removed in 1947 as the city grew. In the oil era many residents moved to new districts, and efforts to preserve the historic area began in the 1970s. After its inscription on the UNESCO list in 2014, 56 historic buildings at risk of collapse were stabilised under a project launched in 2019.

Religious Significance

No special religious virtue is narrated for Jeddah; its importance comes from having been, for centuries, the gateway for pilgrims travelling to Makkah. Pilgrims arriving by sea landed here and set out from here towards Makkah. For this reason the city's culture bears the traces of pilgrims and merchants from every corner of the world.

Today, too, most visitors to Makkah pass through Jeddah. Touring Al-Balad offers a chance to see up close the historical journey of Hajj and Umrah, and the architecture and daily life of the townspeople who served the pilgrims.

Etiquette and Tips

  • The best time is late afternoon and the cool of the evening, when the narrow streets and souqs are most lively.
  • Respect the historic buildings; do not enter buildings under restoration or touch the wooden rawasheen.
  • The streets are narrow and the ground is uneven in places; wear comfortable walking shoes.
  • When prayer time comes, you can pray at one of the historic mosques, such as the al-Shafi'i Mosque.
  • Choose modest clothing that respects local customs.
  • Ask the price before buying in the souq; bargaining is possible.

Visiting Note

Saudi regulations

Some historic buildings and museums (such as Naseef House) may be closed for restoration or events, or may require a ticket; opening hours depend on the Saudi Ministry of Culture's Historic Jeddah programme. Jeddah is outside the Haram; if you are going on to Makkah for Umrah, confirm where and when to enter Ihram with your group guide.

Regulations may change; check the latest rules before your visit.
