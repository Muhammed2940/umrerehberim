Al-Bay'ah Mosque

مسجد البيعة

The historic mosque where the Muslims of Madinah pledged allegiance to the Prophet at Aqabah

Overview

Al-Bay'ah Mosque stands at the Makkah-side entrance of Mina, a few hundred metres north-west of Jamrat al-Aqabah (the large jamrah), in a small valley at the foot of Mount Thabir also called Shi'b al-Ansar. It was built to commemorate the pledges of loyalty that the Muslims of Madinah gave the Prophet here in 621 and 622, known as the Pledges of Aqabah. It is also called the Aqabah Mosque.

It is a small, simple mosque consisting of an open courtyard and two rows of arcades on the qiblah side, covering about 500 square metres. Inscriptions on the qiblah wall and the southern wall show that it was built by the Abbasid caliph Abu Ja'far al-Mansur. It is surrounded by an iron fence.

History

In the early years of his prophethood, the Prophet visited the tribes that came to Makkah in the pilgrimage season and invited them to Islam. In 620 he met six men of the Khazraj tribe from Yathrib (Madinah) at Aqabah, and they accepted Islam.

The next year, in 621, twelve men, ten from Khazraj and two from Aws, pledged allegiance to the Prophet at the same place. They promised not to associate anything with God, not to steal, not to commit adultery, not to kill their children and not to disobey him in what is right. This is the First Pledge of Aqabah. The Prophet sent Mus'ab ibn Umayr with them to Madinah to teach them the religion.

In the pilgrimage season of 622, seventy-five Muslims of Madinah, two of them women, met the Prophet secretly at night at Aqabah. His uncle al-Abbas ibn Abd al-Muttalib was with him. The people of Madinah promised to protect the Prophet as they protected their own lives, property and families, and twelve representatives (naqibs) were chosen from among them. After this Second Pledge of Aqabah the Muslims began to emigrate to Madinah.

To keep this memory alive, the Abbasid caliph Abu Ja'far al-Mansur built a mosque here in 144 AH (761). It was repaired in later centuries, including under the Abbasid caliph al-Mustansir in the 13th century. Over time it became hidden behind the hill of Aqabah. In 1428 AH (2007), when the hill was removed during the expansion works around the Jamarat, it came to light again. In 2022 it was included in the second phase of Saudi Arabia's project to restore historic mosques.

Key Events

  1. 620

    Six men of the Khazraj accepted Islam at Aqabah.

  2. 621

    First Pledge of Aqabah: twelve men of Madinah pledged; Mus'ab ibn Umayr was sent to Madinah.

  3. 622

    Second Pledge of Aqabah: seventy-five people of Madinah, two of them women, pledged; the Hijrah followed.

  4. 144 AH (761)

    The Abbasid caliph Abu Ja'far al-Mansur built the mosque.

  5. 1428 AH (2007)

    The mosque became visible again when the hill of Aqabah was removed during the Jamarat works.

Religious Significance

The Pledges of Aqabah laid the foundation of the Muslim community in Madinah and of the Hijrah. After these promises the Muslims, who were under pressure in Makkah, found a safe home. The Ansar's readiness to give their lives and wealth for God and His Messenger is one of the finest examples of loyalty and sacrifice.

No separate virtue is reported for this mosque. It is fitting here to remember the faith of the Ansar and their faithfulness to their word, to pray for them and to reflect on the promises we ourselves have made. Ubadah ibn al-Samit, who was present at Aqabah, described the promises given at the First Pledge (al-Bukhari, Manaqib al-Ansar; Muslim, al-Hudud).

Etiquette and Tips

  • The mosque is in Mina; it is usually shown from outside on group tours to Mina.
  • If the gates are open, you may pray the obligatory prayer or tahiyyat al-masjid; there is no prayer specific to this place.
  • Do not touch the historic walls and inscriptions, and do not write on them.
  • During the Hajj days the area carries heavy pedestrian traffic; do not plan a visit then.

Visiting Note

Saudi regulations

The mosque is fenced with iron railings and has been part of the historic mosques restoration project since 2022. Whether the gates are open varies; most visitors see it from outside. Vehicle access to Mina may be restricted as the Hajj season approaches.

Regulations may change; check the latest rules before your visit.
