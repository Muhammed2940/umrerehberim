Mount Abu Qubays

جبل أبي قبيس

The historic mountain rising just east of the Kaaba, at whose foot lies Safa

Overview

Abu Qubays is a small mountain rising about 100 metres east of the Kaaba. The valley of Makkah lies between Abu Qubays to the east and the Qu'ayqi'an mountains to the west. The hill of Safa, where sa'y begins, lies at its foot, and Dar al-Arqam, where the first Muslims gathered, once stood on its slope.

In pre-Islamic times it was also called “al-Amin”, and it was described with names such as Shaykh al-Jibal (the elder of the mountains). After the 1980s the whole mountain was expropriated; royal palaces were built on top of it, and tunnels connecting al-Masjid al-Haram with al-Aziziyyah and Mina were cut beneath it. Today, seen from the Safa side, the mountain lies beneath the palace buildings rising east of the Haram, and it is not open for visitors to climb.

History

According to al-Azraqi, the mountain takes its name from a man called Abu Qubays who tried to build on it; there are other accounts of the origin of its name as well. It is reported that, being safe from floods, it was home to the first inhabitants of Makkah before they settled in the valley, and that the ascetics and worshippers of Makkah used to retreat here.

The sources contain several narrations about this mountain. According to them, the Black Stone was entrusted to this mountain during the Flood of Noah and was taken from here when Abraham built the Kaaba, which is why the mountain was called “al-Amin” (the trustworthy). It is also narrated that Abraham climbed this mountain when he called people to the pilgrimage. According to a narration traced to Ibn Abbas and Ibn Mas'ud, the miracle of the splitting of the moon was seen over this mountain. Some of these reports are weak and should not be presented as established facts.

In a hadith narrated by al-Bukhari, the Prophet describes the hardest day he experienced, on his return from Ta'if. The angel of the mountains told him that, if he wished, he would bring the two mountains surrounding Makkah (al-Akhshabayn) down upon the idolaters; the Prophet declined, saying he hoped that from their descendants would come people who worship God alone (al-Bukhari, Bad' al-Khalq, 7). Most scholars say that one of these two mountains is Abu Qubays.

When al-Hajjaj besieged Abdullah ibn al-Zubayr in Makkah in 73 AH (692), he placed one of his catapults on this mountain. On its summit stood a very old mosque known as the Mosque of Ibrahim; it was rebuilt by a Muslim from India in 1257 AH (1841) and survived until the 1980s. In 1837 the first Sanusi lodge was also founded on this mountain. After the 1980s the mountain was entirely expropriated and palaces were built on it.

Key Events

  1. Makkan period

    The first open call to Islam was made on Safa; Dar al-Arqam stood on the mountain's slope.

  2. 73 AH (692)

    Al-Hajjaj placed one of his catapults on the mountain while besieging Abdullah ibn al-Zubayr.

  3. 1257 AH (1841)

    The old Mosque of Ibrahim on the summit was rebuilt.

  4. 1980s

    The mountain was expropriated; palaces were built on it and tunnels beneath it.

Religious Significance

No special virtue is reported for climbing Abu Qubays or worshipping there. Its importance lies in being the mountain closest to the Kaaba and a witness to many events of the early years of Islam.

When beginning sa'y at Safa, remembering that the Prophet called his people to the oneness of God on this hill, and that even after being stoned at Ta'if he wished for their guidance rather than their destruction, teaches patience and mercy.

Etiquette and Tips

  • The mountain cannot be climbed; it is seen from the Safa side and the eastern courtyard of the Haram.
  • Avoid photographing the palace and security areas.
  • When telling the narrations about the mountain, do not present the weak ones as established facts.

Visiting Note

Saudi regulations

The top of the mountain is reserved for the royal palace and closed to visitors. The tunnels beneath it are used for vehicle and pedestrian traffic. The mountain can be seen from the Mas'a building, where the hill of Safa is, and from the eastern courtyard of the Haram.

Regulations may change; check the latest rules before your visit.
