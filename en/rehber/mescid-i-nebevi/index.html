The Prophet's Mosque (al-Masjid an-Nabawi)

المسجد النبوي الشريف

Ar-Rawdah, the Prophet's resting place and the Green Dome

Overview

The Prophet's Mosque was built by the Prophet Muhammad himself right after his emigration to Madinah, and for the rest of his life it served as the centre of worship, teaching, consultation and public affairs. According to most scholars it ranks second in virtue only to al-Masjid al-Haram. The Prophet, Abu Bakr and Umar are buried in the south-eastern corner of the mosque, in the chamber known as al-Hujrah ash-Sharifah, over which rises the Green Dome (al-Qubbah al-Khadra).

The most precious part of the mosque is ar-Rawdah, the area between the Prophet's house and his pulpit, which a hadith describes as “one of the gardens of Paradise”. Today this area of roughly 22 × 15 metres is marked by white and green carpets. It contains the Prophet's mihrab, the marble pulpit sent by the Ottoman Sultan Murad III, and columns each recalling a particular memory (the columns of Hannanah, Aishah, Repentance/Abu Lubabah, as-Sarir, the Guard and the Delegations). After the latest major expansions, the mosque and its courtyards can hold hundreds of thousands of worshippers at once.

History

When the Prophet entered Madinah on 12 Rabi al-Awwal (September 622), he stayed in the house of Abu Ayyub al-Ansari, the house nearest to the spot where his camel al-Qaswa knelt. The plot where the camel knelt, used for drying dates, belonged to two orphans named Sahl and Suhayl; the Prophet bought it for ten dinars. He laid the first stone of the foundation himself and worked alongside the Emigrants and the Helpers. This first mosque, with mud-brick walls and a roof partly covered with palm fronds, was completed in April 623. Rooms for the Prophet's wives were built against its eastern wall; Aishah's room later became known as al-Hujrah ash-Sharifah. When the qiblah was turned towards the Kaaba, the shaded area on the northern side became the Suffah, where Companions who had no family or home lived and studied.

The mosque was enlarged in the Prophet's own lifetime after the return from Khaybar (628); Uthman bought the neighbouring plots for this purpose. It was expanded again under Umar (638) and Uthman (649-650), who had it rebuilt with stone and lime. By order of the Umayyad caliph al-Walid ibn Abd al-Malik, the governor of Madinah, Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz, rebuilt the mosque between 707 and 710; the Prophet's chamber was then brought inside the mosque, and a minaret and a niche-shaped mihrab were added for the first time. Under the Abbasid caliph al-Mahdi (777-782) the mosque was extended to the north.

After the fires of 1256 and 1481 the mosque was restored by the Mamluk sultans, especially Qaytbay; the first dome over the Prophet's grave was also built in the Mamluk period. The Ottomans carried out many repairs, beginning with Suleiman the Magnificent. Under Mahmud II (1817-1837) the dome over the grave was rebuilt in stone, covered with lead and painted green; this is today's Green Dome. Sultan Abdulmejid had the mosque completely rebuilt between 1849 and 1861; the calligraphy of Abdullah Zuhdi and the elegant domed Ottoman section can still be seen in the old part of the mosque.

The first Saudi expansion took place between 1949 and 1955. With the great expansion of 1984-1994 the mosque surrounded the old building on three sides, and sliding domes and ten minarets were added. Later, giant umbrellas were installed to shade the courtyards.

Key Events

  1. 622

    The Prophet arrived in Madinah; the plot was bought and construction of the mosque began.

  2. 623-624

    The first building was completed; 16-17 months after the Hijrah the qiblah was turned from Jerusalem to the Kaaba and the Suffah came into being.

  3. 628

    The mosque was enlarged after Khaybar; the first three-step pulpit of tamarisk wood was made (628 or 629).

  4. 632

    The Prophet passed away and was buried in Aishah's room, which came to be known as al-Hujrah ash-Sharifah.

  5. 707-710

    Rebuilding by Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz: the Prophet's chamber was included in the mosque; the first minaret and mihrab were built.

  6. 1481-1483

    After a fire caused by lightning, the Mamluk Sultan Qaytbay restored the mosque.

  7. 1837 / 1849-1861

    The dome was rebuilt and painted green under Mahmud II; Sultan Abdulmejid had the mosque rebuilt from the ground up.

  8. 1984-1994

    The largest expansion in its history: the mosque reached about 98,000 m², and about 400,000 m² with its courtyards.

Religious Significance

The Prophet stated that his mosque is one of the three mosques to which one may set out on a journey (al-Bukhari). Authentic hadiths establish that a prayer in this mosque is better than a thousand prayers elsewhere, except al-Masjid al-Haram.

The virtue of ar-Rawdah rests on the hadith stating that the area between the Prophet's house and his pulpit is one of the gardens of Paradise. Scholars have understood this either literally or as meaning that worship performed there leads to Paradise. The believer who comes to Madinah regards praying in the mosque and sending blessings and greetings upon the Prophet as the essence of the visit.

Etiquette and Tips

  • Enter the mosque in a state of wudu, in clean and modest clothes, with your right foot and the supplication for entering a mosque; first pray two rak'ahs of tahiyyat al-masjid.
  • To greet the Prophet, visitors usually enter from Bab as-Salam and pass in a queue in front of the Prophet's chamber. Give your greeting briefly and respectfully without raising your voice, then greet Abu Bakr and Umar.
  • Do not push anyone in the crowd and follow the directions of the staff. Make your supplications facing the qiblah.
  • Do not speak loudly in the mosque; avoid video calls and lengthy photography.
  • While in Madinah, try to pray the daily prayers in congregation at the Prophet's Mosque as much as possible, and send abundant blessings upon the Prophet.
  • Put your shoes on the racks or in a bag you carry, noting the gate number; use gate numbers as meeting points.

Visiting Note

Saudi regulations

Praying in ar-Rawdah requires a free permit (appointment) from the Nusuk app. Men and women are admitted at separate times through separate gates; each visit is limited to about 10 minutes, and an annual limit on permits may apply. Visitors are not allowed to stop when greeting the Prophet in front of the chamber; the line keeps moving and exits towards the Baqi gate.

Regulations may change; check the latest rules before your visit.
